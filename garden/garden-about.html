My Four-Year Garden Blueprint: Planning for Year-Round Harvests

If you’ve ever dealt with stubborn soil pests or tired garden beds, you know that planning ahead makes all the difference. For my 2027–2030 garden setup here in Stockton, California, I put together a four-year crop rotation blueprint designed to keep the soil healthy, prevent disease, and keep fresh produce coming all year long.

The Four Core Groups

  1. Group Nightshades — The heavy hitters of the summer garden, including German Johnson and cherry tomatoes, California Wonder and Anaheim peppers, and Yukon Gold and Red Pontiac potatoes.
  2. Group Cucurbits — My vining and sprawling warm-weather favorites: Sugar Baby watermelon, Hearts of Gold cantaloupe, Black Beauty zucchini, Marketmore cucumbers, and spaghetti squash.
  3. Group Brassicas — Cool-season staples and quick growers like Waltham broccoli, Snowball cauliflower, Roquette arugula, and French Breakfast radishes.
  4. Group Legumes — Soil-building climbers including Sugar Anne snap peas and Kentucky Wonder pole beans.

How the 86-Square-Foot Layout Works

Instead of guessing what to plant where each spring, five of my six permanent raised beds rotate through four major plant families—Nightshades, Cucurbits, Brassicas, and Legumes. By moving each family to a new bed every year, no crop returns to the same spot until the cycle resets in 2031.

To make the most of a smaller footprint, vertical arches and trellises handle climbing crops, while my sixth bed stays dedicated year-round to fast-growing salad greens and root crops like carrots, beets, lettuce, and spinach. Perennial herbs and trailing berries (blackberries and boysenberries trained on cattle panels) live in permanent containers outside the main rotation.

Keeping the Soil Thriving

A good plan only works if you stick to the basics between seasons:

  • Log by plant family: Tracking what grew where keeps the four-year rotation on schedule.
  • Feed the beds: Topping off beds with an inch of cured compost between major crop transitions rebuilds nutrients naturally.
  • Stay ahead of disease: Sanitizing trellises and tools—and trashing diseased foliage instead of composting it—protects next season's harvest.
